1. Flatness

From inside the cave,

the world is nothing but a wall of shadows. The people chained there do not see objects, light, or depth. They see only silhouettes moving across a flat surface, and from these shifting outlines they construct their understanding of reality.

At times,

the creature appears to get smaller, its form shrinking across the wall. Later, it seems to stretch upward, as though sprouting a second limb. to the prisoners, these changes are not illusions. They believe the being itself is transforming. Since they cannot turn around, they assume that what they see is the full truth of what exists.

This is what Nick Sousains describes as Flatness in Unflattening: a condition in which our understanding is limited to a single surface or perspective. Flatness is not simply about two-dimensional space, it is a way of thinking that reduces layered realities into one visible plane. The prisoner's worldview is flat because they mistake projection for true form. They cannot percieve depth, overlap, or hidden structure. When the shadow shrinks, they believe the creature itself is getting smaller. When it grows, they believe its body has changed. They reduce complexity into a single explanation because they lack access to the dimesnsions beyond the wall. Their reality is incomplete.

2. Seeing Double

From outside the cave, the scene appears entirely different. There is a light source, a wall, and between them stand two seperate objects. The people outside cannot see how the prisoners interpret the shadows inside, but they can see the full mechanism that produces them.

When we pull one object away from the other, the single shadow seperates into two distinct forms on the wall. When we bring them back together again, their outlines overlap and merge into what appears to be one unified creature. Nothing about either object changes in its structure. What changes is their spatial relationship to one another and to the light.

This perspective shows what Sousainis calls Seeing Double. Seeing double is the ability to hold more than one perspective at once to understand both the projection and the system producing it. It is dimensional thinking. The observers outside the cave can see the two independant objects and the shadow they create together. They recognize that the unity perceived inside is the result of alingment, not identity. Events outside the cave like the movement of objects in space, directly cause the shadows inside to merge, shrink, or divide. The prisoners experience these shifts as changes in a single being, while the observers outside understand them as interactions between multiple forms. Seeing double does not reject the shadow; it situates it within a larger system. It acknowledges that what appears singular may in fact be layered and dependant on hidden factors. Where flatness compresses reality into one surface, seeing double expands it, proving that truth requires looking beyond what first appears.
