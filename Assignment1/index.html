1. Flatness

From Inside the cave,

The world is nothing but a wall of shadows. The people chained there cannot perceive objects, light, or depth in any meaningful sense. They are limited to observing a two-dimensional projection, a simplified and flattened representation of a far more complex reality. To them, shadows are beings, movements are intentions, and shifting shapes are transformations of the creatures themselves. Their interpretations are entirely based on appearances, which are constantly in motion and contingent upon factors they cannot see. Every movement across the wall becomes a story, a meaningful event, or a creatures' change, even though these interpretations are disconnected from the physical processes creating the images.

At times,

the creature appears to get smaller, its form shrinking across the wall. Later, it seems to stretch upward, as though sprouting a second limb. To the prisoners, these changes are not illusions; they are the full and undeniable truth of the world. They believe the being itself is transforming. Since they cannot turn around, they assume that what they see is the full truth of what exists. Each shape, movement, or shift is interpreted as a part of one object, rather than a result of the spatial relationships outside their view

This is what Nick Sousains describes as Flatness in Unflattening: a condition in which our understanding is limited to a single surface or perspective. Flatness is not simply about two-dimensional space, it is a way of thinking that reduces layered realities into one visible plane. The prisoner's worldview is flat because they mistake projection for true form, shadows for objects, and surface for reality. They cannot percieve depth, overlap, or hidden causes behind what appears infront of them. When the shadow shrinks, they believe the creature itself is getting smaller. When it grows, they believe its body has changed. They reduce complexity into a single explanation because they lack access to the dimesnsions beyond the wall. Their reality is incomplete.

2. Seeing Double

From outside the cave, the same scene appears entirely different. Observers see that there is a light source, a wall, and between them stand two seperate objects. Unlike the prisoners, these observers are aware of the causal mechanism producing the shadows. They can trace how the objects' positions and spatial relationships interact with light to produce the moving shadows on the wall. What appears to the prisoners as a single creature is now revealed to be the product of alignment, distance, and perspective. The shadows are understood not as objects themselves but as results of a larger network of interactions.

When we pull one object away from the other, the single shadow seperates revealing two distinct forms on the wall. When we bring them back together again, their outlines overlap and merge into what appears to be one unified creature. Nothing about either object changes in its structure. What changes is their spatial relationship to one another and to the light. This shows that what appears singular may actually be the result of an intersection of multiple realities and that perception relies on alignment and context. The outside perspective clarifies the difference between appearance and structure.

This perspective shows what Sousainis calls Seeing Double. Seeing double is the ability to hold more than one perspective at once to understand both the projection and the system producing it. It is a way of dimensional thinking that takes into account layers, interactions, and nuance. The observers outside the cave can see the two independant objects and the shadow they create together. They recognize that the unity perceived inside the cave is the result of alingment, not the merging of a single creature. This cognitive skill allows one to hold multiple perspectives at once, recognizing that reality may appear different depending on one's point of view.

By looking at both perspectives, the flat, inside view of the prisoners and the dimensional, outside view of the observers, we understand the limitations and possibilities of perception. Events outside the cave, like the movement of objects or changes in spacing, directly produce the idea that the prisoners interpret as transformation. Understanding this system allows us to explain the shadows as effects of physical change, rather than as magical changes in form. Seeing double shows that knowledge requires attention to context, structure, and hidden layers, while flatness limits understanding to a single surface.
